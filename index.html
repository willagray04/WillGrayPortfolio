A cost database a builder can actually price a house from

Sendero Homes/Custom homebuilding/2026

Sendero’s entire cost history lived in one spreadsheet. I turned it into a database, then gave Claude a way to query it — so a new home gets priced against real comparable jobs instead of a rule-of-thumb range.

The build has two halves: a PostgreSQL schema hosted on Supabase that models clients, projects, a reusable cost catalog, versioned estimates and historical job costs; and a TypeScript MCP server that exposes that data to Claude as callable tools.

  • Migrated the master spreadsheet into eight historical homes broken across 40 cost categories and nine owner-allowance items, each with cost per heated-cooled and per total-covered square foot.
  • Recency-weighted averages so a 2025 job counts for more than a 2022 one — with a separate flag for whether a job’s date is a completion or an excavation start, since three jobs were still in progress.
  • A specs table built from the plan sets — framing type, roof system, exterior envelope, window package, finish tier, specialty items — so a new plan matches the closest real comparable rather than the portfolio average.
  • Tools exposed to Claude: list and read projects, search the cost catalog, pull cost and allowance averages, draft an estimate, and project a budget for a new build.

02

Funding drainage that a small town can’t otherwise afford

Pine Island, Texas/URPN 401, Group 8/Spring 2026

A 58-page implementation roadmap for standing up a municipal stormwater utility — a dedicated, recurring funding source for a town whose drainage problems had no budget line to fix them.

The core argument is that fees should follow runoff. Charging against impervious surface area means a property’s bill tracks what it actually contributes to the problem, which is both fairer and easier to defend at a public hearing.

  • Four case studies of cities that had already implemented stormwater utilities, mined for what worked in fee development, ordinance drafting, and budgeting.
  • An ERU-based rate structure: residential properties pay a flat monthly fee around $4.00 per Equivalent Residential Unit; commercial properties scale by measured impervious area.
  • A draft ordinance establishing the utility under Public Works — on-site drainage requirements for new development, plan review, rate adjustment provisions, and an appeals process for property owners.
  • An implementation timeline and financial plan that ring-fences revenue for stormwater work only: maintenance, flood mitigation, and long-range planning.

03

Volcanic hazards, and who lives close to them

Oregon Cascades/GEOG 390, Lab 2/October 2025

A reference hazards map built so it answers an exposure question, not just a geological one: how many people sit near an active volcano, and how far are they from a hospital?

Three layers do the work together — county population density underneath, eruption history on top of it, and the response network of highways and hospitals over both.

  • Population density as a five-class choropleth (0.72–1,600 people per square mile) on a sequential tan-to-brown ramp, kept deliberately subordinate so it never competes with the hazard symbols.
  • Graduated triangles for eruption count at one, five, and ten events, with the named features labelled: Hood, Belknap, Newberry, Crater Lake, North Sister Field, and Sand Mountain Field.
  • Hospitals drawn as a distinct cross glyph and highways split by class, so emergency infrastructure reads as its own system rather than as basemap texture.
  • Full map furniture: inset locator, graduated scale bar in miles, north arrow, and complete source attribution.

Volcanic Hazards Map — Oregon. Built in ArcGIS Pro. Sources: Esri, TomTom, Garmin, FAO, NOAA, USGS, OpenStreetMap contributors.

Is population growth moving people into tornado country?

United States, 1950–2022/GEOG 232 final project/Fall 2024

A bivariate thematic map and a published ArcGIS StoryMap, setting 72 years of tornado tracks against county population density for 2010 and 2020 — written for emergency managers and planners rather than for a general audience.

The map found the heaviest overlap between dense population and recurring tornado activity across the Midwest and South, which is an argument for where warning infrastructure and preparedness funding should concentrate.

  • Tornado tracks colour-coded by intensity over a county-level density surface, so frequency and exposure can be read in a single pass.
  • A real review cycle: three reviewers each gave three strengths and three criticisms of the draft — symbol density overwhelming the choropleth, a missing legend, and no layer control among them.
  • A documented revision pass responding to each critique: added the legend, replaced and repositioned the supporting imagery, and expanded the written analysis of how density and tornado frequency correlate.
  • Reasoned pushback where warranted — the point-density criticism was answered rather than accepted, since the full extent had to stay legible at the zoomed-out view.

Setting the control everything else gets measured from

Geodetic control & UAS survey/GEOG 352 and field work/2024–2025

Before a site plan, a volume calculation, or a drone flight means anything, something has to be tied to a known coordinate. This is the field half of my work: establishing that control, then flying and drafting off it.

  • Four geodetic control points established with a Leica GS15 receiver — static sessions processed through NOAA OPUS against the CORS network, real-time positions pulled from TxDOT’s VRS network, and NGS NCAT used to compare coordinates across datums.
  • Documented to TxDOT standards — reported under Section 2 of the TxDOT Survey Manual, with the survey performed under the supervision of a Texas Registered Professional Land Surveyor.
  • Five sets of nadir UAS imagery — roughly 428 frames and 2.0 GB across Wolf Creek, Lampasas, Galveston, and two further sites — paired with surveyed ground-control files for photogrammetric registration.
  • Drafted output: a topographic plat with spot elevations, contours, title block, graphic scale, and north arrow, produced from the surveyed points.
  • Trained on the Drone2Map → ArcGIS Pro workflow for structure-from-motion DTM and DSM generation and retention-basin volumetrics.

A companion study with two classmates tested how far consumer hardware can be trusted: surveying five campus landmarks with Field Maps on iPhone and comparing against ArcGIS Pro reference points returned an average positional deviation under one metre.

Top: nadir frame from the Wolf Creek flight set, ground control target visible mid-field. Bottom: topographic plat drafted from surveyed points — 1″ = 75′, spot elevations in feet.
