Sendero Homes · Austin, Texas · May–August 2026

An automated construction cost estimation tool

Built during my internship to streamline budget creation and project cost analysis — turning a spreadsheet of past jobs into something a new build can be priced against.

The problem

Every custom home Sendero had built was recorded, but the record lived in one master spreadsheet. The numbers were all there and none of them were reachable: to price a new build you either dug through old jobs by hand or fell back on a rule-of-thumb cost per square foot.

A rule of thumb is a single number standing in for a lot of variation. Two homes of the same size can differ enormously depending on whether there is a pool, an elevator, structural steel, or a steep lot that needs retaining walls. The company had the evidence to be more precise than that; it just was not in a usable shape.

My role

I developed the tool. That covered designing the database schema, migrating the historical job data into it, writing the estimation logic, and building the interface that exposes it.

  • Applied construction cost estimation and project budgeting knowledge to design estimation logic that stays accurate as more jobs are added.
  • Collaborated with the estimating and marketing teams to align the tool with how the company actually works, and to support business development.

Data and method

Data

  • Eight historical homes from the company master spreadsheet, each with its total construction cost and heated-cooled, covered, garage, and patio square footage.
  • Forty cost categories per job — permits, foundations, framing, plumbing, electrical, roofing, cabinets, landscaping and the rest — with both dollar amounts and each category's share of the total.
  • Nine owner-allowance items tracked separately, because things like cabinets and pools vary by client choice rather than by build method.
  • Cost-driving specs pulled from each job's plan set: framing type, roof system, exterior envelope, window package, interior finish tier, and specialty items.

Method

The jobs go into a PostgreSQL database rather than a spreadsheet, which makes the history queryable. A new budget is then projected by taking a cost basis and distributing it across the forty categories according to the share each one has historically taken.

Two refinements matter more than they sound. The first is recency weighting — a 2025 job should count for more than a 2022 one, because material and labour prices move. The second is a flag for whether a job's date is a completion or an excavation start, since three of the eight were still in progress and treating their dates as completions would have skewed the weighting.

The specs table is what lifts this above an average. Rather than pricing a new plan against the mean of eight homes, it can be matched to the closest real comparable — the one with the same framing type, roof system, and finish tier.

What I found

The structure of a custom-home budget is less intuitive than it looks. Across the eight jobs, exterior structures is the single largest category at 10.6% of total spend — ahead of windows and doors at 8.1%, cabinets at 7.7%, and framing materials at 7.0%. The things people assume dominate a build do not.

Putting that in front of someone changes the conversation. A client weighing an outdoor structure against a finish upgrade can see which decision actually moves the budget, and by how much, before anything is drawn.

The other finding was about the data itself. Three of the eight jobs were still in progress, and their recorded dates were excavation starts rather than completions. Treating those as finished jobs would have quietly distorted every recency-weighted average the tool produced — a small modelling detail with disproportionate consequences.

What I would do differently

Access control from the start. As built, every tool can see everything. Before this holds real financial data for a whole team, it needs a decision about who should see pricing versus financials versus only their own projects, and filtering by the calling user's role. Retrofitting permissions is harder than designing for them.

More jobs before trusting the weighting. Eight is enough to show the shape of a budget and not enough to be confident about any one category. Two of the forty appear in only a subset of jobs, so their averages rest on very little. I would be clearer in the interface about which figures are well-evidenced and which are not.

Somewhere persistent to run. It runs locally, which means it is useful to whoever starts it rather than to the team. Getting it onto a small always-on server was the obvious next milestone.
